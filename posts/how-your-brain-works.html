How Your Brain Actually Works

September 2, 2026 · 7 min read

Your brain is running right now, and it's doing an almost unbelievable amount of work to let you read this sentence. About 86 billion neurons are firing, rewiring, and passing signals to each other, all inside something that weighs roughly as much as a small cauliflower. Here's what's actually happening in there.

The neuron: your brain's basic unit

A neuron is a single nerve cell built to do one job: pass along an electrical and chemical signal. It has three main parts. Dendrites reach out like branches to receive signals from other neurons. The cell body processes that input. The axon is a long fiber that carries the signal onward, sometimes over a meter long in the case of neurons running down your spinal cord.

How neurons actually talk to each other

Neurons don't physically touch. Between the end of one neuron and the start of the next is a tiny gap called a synapse. When an electrical signal reaches the end of an axon, it triggers the release of chemical messengers called neurotransmitters, things like dopamine, serotonin, and glutamate. These chemicals drift across the gap and lock onto receptors on the next neuron, which can either excite it into firing or calm it down.

This chemical handoff is what most psychiatric medications actually target: adjusting how much of a given neurotransmitter is available in that gap.

Why does this matter for learning?

Every time a connection between two neurons gets used, it gets slightly stronger. Neuroscientists sum this up as "neurons that fire together, wire together." Practicing a skill is, physically, the repeated strengthening of a specific chain of synapses.

The brain's major regions

Zoom out from single neurons and the brain organizes itself into regions with different jobs. Hover or tap each shape below.

The frontal lobe handles reasoning, decision-making, and impulse control, it's also the last region to fully mature, often not until your mid-20s. The parietal lobe integrates sensory information and spatial awareness. The temporal lobe handles hearing and plays a major role in forming memories. The occipital lobe, at the very back, is almost entirely dedicated to processing vision. Below all of it, the cerebellum fine-tunes movement and balance.

How a memory actually forms

When you experience something new, a structure deep in the temporal lobe called the hippocampus acts like a temporary save file. Over the following hours and days, especially during sleep, that memory gets gradually transferred into longer-term storage across the cortex, the brain's outer layer.

Why sleep matters so much for memory

During deep sleep, the hippocampus essentially replays the day's events, strengthening the connections tied to that memory so it can be moved into permanent storage. Cutting sleep short interrupts this transfer, which is a big part of why studying all night before an exam is often counterproductive.

Neuroplasticity: your brain keeps changing

For a long time, scientists assumed the adult brain was fixed. We now know that's wrong. The brain continues forming new connections, and in a few regions, even new neurons, throughout life. This is called neuroplasticity. It's why learning a new language, an instrument, or a skill at 40 or 70 is genuinely possible: the underlying wiring is still adaptable.

Every thought you have is a pattern of electrical and chemical signals moving through this network, billions of tiny handoffs happening every second, forming the entire experience of being you.
