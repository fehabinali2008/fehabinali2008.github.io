Why the sky is blue (and why sunsets aren't)

September 2, 2026 · 4 min read

Sunlight looks white, but it's actually made of every color mixed together. When it hits our atmosphere, it runs into countless tiny gas molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen, that are far smaller than the wavelength of light itself.

This is where a phenomenon called Rayleigh scattering comes in. Shorter wavelengths of light, like blue and violet, get scattered by these molecules far more than longer wavelengths like red and orange. Blue light gets bounced around the sky in every direction, so no matter where you look, some of it reaches your eyes.

So why isn't the sky violet?

Violet light actually scatters even more than blue. But two things work against it: the sun emits less violet light to begin with, and our eyes are simply less sensitive to it. The combination means blue wins out as the color we perceive.

Then why do sunsets turn orange and red?

At sunset, sunlight travels through a much thicker slice of atmosphere to reach you. By the time it does, most of the blue light has already scattered away in other directions, leaving the longer wavelengths, orange and red, to dominate what finally reaches your eyes.

Same physics, same sky, completely different color, just depending on how much atmosphere the light has to fight through.
